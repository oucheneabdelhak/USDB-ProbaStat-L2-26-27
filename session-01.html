1Why statistics and probability?

We observe a part (sample, logs, A/B test) to say something about a whole (population, future traffic, production system), and the observations are affected by variability no engineering can remove.

Descriptive statistics
Condenses observations into a table, a graph and a few numbers.
Probability
Models the variability itself: possible outcomes, events, degree of belief.

Two halves of the same method: descriptive statistics without probability cannot generalise; probability without descriptive statistics never meets data.

2What statistics is

Definition
Statistics is the set of methods used to analyse and process sets of observations (data) in order to facilitate decision-making under uncertainty. It rests on mathematics and makes extensive use of computer tools.
Example
Average latency of a service, histogram of dataset sizes, testing whether two versions of an interface differ.
Remark: statistics vs a statistic
Statistics is the science. A statistic is a number computed from a sample: a mean, a percentage, a correlation coefficient.

4The two parts of the course

Descriptive statistics
Organises, summarises and describes a set of observations actually collected, without extrapolating beyond them.

Frequency table of editors, histogram of dataset sizes, mean and standard deviation of latency.

Probability
Models a random experiment: the set of outcomes, the events, and a measure of the likelihood of each. The foundation of every inference from sample to population.

P(request served in < 50 ms), P(false positive | alert).

Session 1 — Population, characters, and the frequency distribution of a qualitative character

Today you will be able to:
  • distinguish population, individual, sample, census and poll;
  • classify any character (nominal, ordinal, discrete, continuous);
  • build a frequency table and draw a pie chart and a bar chart.

The vocabulary is not decorative: every later error of interpretation traces back to one of these distinctions being ignored.

1Population, individual, sample

Definition
Population \(\Omega\): the complete set of units concerned by the study.
Definition
Individual \(\omega\in\Omega\): one member of the population (whatever the study is about).
Definition
Sample: the subset of \(\Omega\) on which observations are actually made, of size \(n\).
Example
Population: all \(1200\) students of a school. Sample: \(80\) of them drawn at random, \(n=80\). In a security study, an individual is a connection attempt, not a person.
Remark
A population need not be finite, nor physical: the set of requests a server could receive is a legitimate population. In Chapter 3, \(\Omega\) will be the set of outcomes of a random experiment.

2Survey, census, poll

Survey
Observing the individuals of a sample to collect data.
Census
Survey where sample = population (exhaustive).
Poll
Survey where the sample is a strict subset.

5Counting: the three fundamental quantities

A qualitative character has \(k\) modalities \(x_1,\dots,x_k\) forming a complete system: each individual belongs to exactly one. Sample size: \(n\).

Absolute frequency
\(n_i\): number of individuals with modality \(x_i\).
Relative frequency
\[f_i=\frac{n_i}{n}\]
Percentage
\[p_i=100\,f_i\]
Proposition \[\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_i=n,\qquad 0\le f_i\le 1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i=1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k}p_i=100\]
Each individual belongs to exactly one modality (the system is complete), so the counts add up to the sample size: \(\sum n_i=n\). Dividing by \(n\) gives \(\sum f_i=1\), and multiplying by 100 gives \(\sum p_i=100\).
Example \(38\) of \(80\) students answered “VS Code”: \(n_{\mathrm{VSC}}=38\), \(f=38/80=0.475\), \(p=47.5\,\%\).
Rounding If frequencies are rounded, adjust the largest one so the total is exactly 1, rather than rounding each independently.

7Pie chart

Definition A disk divided into sectors whose angles are proportional to the frequencies: \[\theta_i=\frac{n_i}{n}\times 360^\circ=\frac{p_i}{100}\times 360^\circ\]
Hover a sector
Sum of angles: 360°
Remark Meaningful only if modalities partition a whole (exhaustive, mutually exclusive). Unreadable beyond ~6 sectors and poor for comparing close frequencies: a communication tool, not an analysis tool.

8Bar chart

Definition Each modality is a vertical rectangle of height proportional to \(n_i\) (or \(f_i\), \(p_i\)); the vertical axis starts at zero.
Remark A truncated axis exaggerates differences — a classic manipulation. By contrast, the order of the modalities is free; sorting by decreasing frequency gives a Pareto chart.

✓Summary of Session 1

  • A study concerns a population, observes a sample through a survey: a census if exhaustive, a poll otherwise.
  • A character is qualitative (nominal / ordinal) or quantitative (discrete / continuous); the type determines the table, graphs and admissible parameters.
  • \(n_i,\; f_i=\dfrac{n_i}{n},\; p_i=100f_i\) with \(\sum n_i=n,\ \sum f_i=1,\ \sum p_i=100\).
  • Pie chart: \(\theta_i=\dfrac{n_i}{n}\times360^\circ\). Bar chart: heights \(\propto n_i\), axis from zero.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the residual modality “Other” (frequencies no longer sum to 1).
  • Truncating the axis of a bar chart.
  • Using a pie chart for non-exhaustive categories.
  • Treating codes (student numbers, HTTP codes) as numbers.
